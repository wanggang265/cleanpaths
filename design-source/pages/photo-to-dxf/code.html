The problem

Photos are the hardest thing to vectorize honestly

  • "A photograph is continuous tone — thousands of colors blending into each other. A cut file is the opposite: a few flat regions with hard edges."
  • "Most converters wave that gap away and let you discover the posterized reality after the download."
  • "And the private photos — family, kids, client work — are exactly the ones you don't want on a stranger's server."
The pipeline

What this converter does with a photo

  1. Reduces the photo to its boldest shapes: flat color regions with clean, closed edges — posterization on purpose, not by accident.

  2. Cleans and upscales first, so the shapes it finds are the subject, not the noise.

  3. Exports real DXF (R2000 with units, R12 compatibility mode) plus SVG — so poster-style art goes straight to the machine that cuts it.

The honest line on photos

Here's the rule, before you convert: photos of flat-color subjects — a logo on a truck door, a sign, a business card — are designed to come out cut-ready. Photos of scenes, people and pets come out stylized: a posterized, graphic-novel version of the original. High contrast and simple backgrounds make strong art; fine photographic detail does not become fine vector detail, and no converter can change that. The preview shows you exactly which result you'll get.

How to get a better DXF from a photo

Chase contrast: a subject that stands out from its background traces into clean regions; a muddy photo traces into mud.

Crop to the subject before you convert — every background element you remove is a shape the tracer doesn't have to guess at.

Expect a poster, not a photograph: a few bold color layers is a good result, and shapes at that scale cut cleanly as decorative pieces.

Photographing a logo or drawing? Flat lighting, a square-on angle, and the highest resolution your phone has.

Is your photo actually a graphic?

There's a useful difference between a photo OF a graphic and a photo of a scene. A phone shot of a logo, a sign or a printed design has flat colors underneath the camera noise — the cleanup pipeline removes that noise before tracing, and the output is designed to be cut-ready. That's also exactly what our JPG to DXF converter is built around. A photo of your dog has no flat colors to recover — it becomes posterized art instead. Both are worth doing. They're just different results, and this page tells you which one you're holding.

FAQ
Will my photo come out as a detailed vector?
No — and be suspicious of any tool that says otherwise. Auto-tracing reduces a photo to flat color regions: the result is a stylized, posterized version of your image, not a photographic reproduction. High-contrast photos make striking poster art; fine detail doesn't survive. The preview shows you all of this before you download.
Can I convert a photo to DXF for laser cutting?
If the photo is of a flat-color graphic — a logo, a sign, a line drawing — the output is designed to be cut-ready, yes. If it's a photograph of a scene or a person, expect stylized poster art: bold shapes that can still cut well as decorative pieces, but not a faithful rendering. Test-cut before production either way. For the full laser workflow — LightBurn import, sizing, R12 decisions — see our PNG to DXF for laser cutting guide.
What photos convert best?
High contrast, simple backgrounds, clear subjects. A logo photographed against the sky converts beautifully; a group photo at a barbecue becomes abstract art. Cropping tight to the subject before you convert is the single biggest improvement you can make.

No. Conversion runs entirely in your browser — there is no upload step. We never receive, see, or store your images or the files you generate. Close the tab and they're gone; we never had them.
