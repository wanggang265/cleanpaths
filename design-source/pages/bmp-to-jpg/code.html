Why convert BMP to JPG?

BMP is one of the oldest image formats still in circulation — the native bitmap of Windows since the beginning, and the default output of plenty of legacy software, scanners, lab instruments and industrial tools. It's also huge: BMP stores pixels with little or no compression, which made sense in its era and makes email attachments cry today. Converting to JPG keeps the picture and sheds the bulk — right in your browser, no upload.

Same picture, a fraction of the size

BMP's bulk comes from storing every pixel almost raw; JPG was designed from day one to compress photographs efficiently. Converting typically shrinks the file dramatically — the exact saving depends on the image, and we won't quote a made-up number. What doesn't change is the direction: from one of the largest common image formats to one of the smallest, with quality the preview lets you judge before you download. Smaller files mean faster uploads, email-friendly attachments, and photo libraries that stop eating your disk.

Where BMP files keep coming from

If you have a BMP today, it almost certainly came from a system that never learned anything newer: old Windows applications and their "Save as image" dialogs, scanner and fax software from the XP era, medical and lab equipment exports, point-of-sale and industrial terminals, screen captures from legacy tools, or a digital camera's uncompressed mode. The files are usually perfectly good images wrapped in an inconvenient container — too big to email, rejected by most upload forms, and slow to open on phones.

A Windows fossil that still works hard

BMP survived because Windows never dropped it: it's still the clipboard's native tongue, still the fallback format of Paint, still what a lot of enterprise and industrial software spits out. None of that is going away — so the practical move isn't to fight the format, it's to convert on demand. Drop the BMP, take the JPG, and the file becomes something every phone, browser, printer and form on earth understands. And because the conversion runs locally, even confidential exports — medical, industrial, internal — never leave your device.

FAQ
Will converting BMP to JPG reduce quality?
BMP is uncompressed, and JPG uses lossy compression — so there is a quality trade by design. For photographs and scans at normal viewing sizes the difference is hard to spot, and the file-size win is large. The preview shows you the result before you download. If you need a mathematically identical copy, keep the BMP as your archive and use the JPG for sharing.
Why is my BMP file so big?
Because BMP stores pixels with little or no compression — every dot of the image is written out almost raw. That's what makes the format simple and universal, and also what makes a single photo many times heavier than the same picture saved as a JPG.
Do BMP files have transparency?
Almost never. Standard BMP has no alpha channel in the form most software writes it, so converting to JPG loses nothing in that respect — neither format carries real transparency. If your image needs a transparent background, that's a job for PNG.
Do you upload or store my files?
No. Conversion runs entirely in your browser — there is no upload step. We never receive, see, or store your images or the files you generate. Close the tab and they're gone; we never had them. This is how the tool is built, not a marketing claim. Details in our Privacy Policy.
